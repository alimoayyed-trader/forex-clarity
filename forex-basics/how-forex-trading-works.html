How Does Forex Trading Work?

Forex trading is the process of exchanging one currency for another. Learn how currency pairs, buy and sell orders, price changes, trading costs and risk fit together in a typical forex trade.

Educational guide · Beginner level

Forex is short for foreign exchange. Currencies are quoted in pairs such as EUR/USD or GBP/JPY. A trader takes a position based on the possibility that one currency will strengthen or weaken relative to the other. Prices can move either way, so a trade can result in a loss as well as a profit.

Important: Forex trading involves substantial risk. Leverage can magnify gains and losses, and you may lose some or all of the money deposited with a provider. This article is educational, not a recommendation to trade.

1. Understand currency pairs

Every forex quote contains two currencies. In EUR/USD, EUR is the base currency and USD is the quote currency. The price tells you how many US dollars are needed to buy one euro.

If EUR/USD is 1.0800, one euro is worth 1.08 US dollars at that quoted price. If the quote rises to 1.0850, the euro has strengthened against the dollar over that interval. If it falls to 1.0750, the euro has weakened against the dollar.

2. Choose whether to buy or sell

  • Buy (go long): you expect the pair’s price to rise. In EUR/USD, that means expecting the euro to strengthen relative to the dollar.
  • Sell (go short): you expect the pair’s price to fall. That means expecting the euro to weaken relative to the dollar.

Being correct about direction is not the only factor. Position size, entry and exit prices, spreads and other fees all affect the final result.

3. A simple trade example

Imagine EUR/USD is quoted at 1.0800 and a trader buys. Later, the quote reaches 1.0850. The market moved 0.0050, or 50 pips for a pair where one pip is typically 0.0001.

This example illustrates price movement, not a guaranteed or typical outcome. If the price moved down instead, the buy position would lose value. A sell position has the opposite directional exposure, while still being affected by costs and risk.

4. Understand trading costs

Spread

The spread is the difference between the bid price (the price at which you can sell) and ask price (the price at which you can buy). A wider spread generally means a higher immediate cost of entering and exiting.

Commission and overnight fees

Some account types charge a separate commission. Depending on the product and provider, overnight financing or swap charges may also apply. Check the provider’s fee schedule and the instrument’s contract specifications.

Slippage

Slippage occurs when an order is filled at a different price from the one expected. It can happen during fast markets, low liquidity or important news. A stop order does not always guarantee execution at the exact stop price.

5. Leverage and margin

Leverage allows a trader to control a position larger than the funds committed as margin. Margin is the collateral required to open and maintain a leveraged position. Requirements vary by instrument, provider and applicable rules.

Leverage magnifies exposure: a small market move can have a large effect relative to the margin used. If the market moves against a leveraged position, losses can accumulate quickly and the provider may close positions when margin requirements are not met.

Do not choose leverage based only on the maximum available. First determine how much you can afford to risk, the distance to your planned invalidation or stop level, and the position size that fits that risk.

6. Orders and trade management

  • Market order: requests execution at the best available price; the final fill may differ from the displayed quote.
  • Limit order: seeks execution at a specified price or better, subject to the provider’s rules and available liquidity.
  • Stop order: activates when its trigger condition is reached; execution may occur at a different price in fast markets.
  • Stop-loss: an instruction intended to exit a position at a defined level. It helps plan risk but does not eliminate slippage or all risk.
  • Take-profit: an instruction intended to close a position at a chosen target, subject to execution conditions.

Before entering a trade, know the reason for entry, what would invalidate the idea, where you plan to exit, and how much you could lose if the trade fails.

7. Manage risk before trading

  • Risk only money you can afford to lose; do not use essential living expenses or borrowed money.
  • Calculate position size from the planned stop distance and the amount you are willing to risk.
  • Remember that gaps, slippage, spreads and fast markets can make actual losses differ from planned losses.
  • Avoid increasing position size just to recover a previous loss.
  • Practise in a demo environment and keep a journal before considering real-money trading.

Risk management cannot guarantee a positive result. It is a way to make risk explicit and avoid taking positions whose potential losses are unacceptable to you.

8. A beginner’s learning checklist

  1. Learn how currency pairs are quoted and what a pip means.
  2. Understand bid, ask, spread and the provider’s fees.
  3. Learn how order types behave, including their limitations.
  4. Understand leverage, margin and the possibility of rapid losses.
  5. Write down an entry idea, invalidation point and exit plan before practising.
  6. Track many practice trades instead of judging a method from one outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Can beginners make money trading forex?

Some traders may make profits, but many retail traders lose money. Outcomes are uncertain and depend on costs, risk control, experience and market conditions. No strategy can guarantee profit.

How much money do I need to start?

Minimum deposits vary by provider and jurisdiction. A low minimum does not make trading low-risk. Understand contract specifications, costs and loss scenarios before depositing money.

Is forex trading open 24 hours a day?

The global forex market operates across major financial centres during the business week, but exact hours, breaks and holiday schedules depend on the instrument and provider. Check the schedule shown by your platform.

Is leverage necessary?

Many retail providers offer leverage, but it is not a shortcut to profit. It increases exposure and can magnify losses. Available leverage and protections vary by jurisdiction and provider.
